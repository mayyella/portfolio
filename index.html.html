01 / BUILDING BETTER IEPS

From Compliance to Instruction

Project type: Professional-learning demonstration • Audience: Special education teachers • Format: 30-minute blended/e-learning concept

Performance goal: Teachers will use assessment information and student needs to create measurable, instructionally meaningful annual goals rather than relying on vague or activity-based statements.
Needs analysis

The performance gap

Information may be available, but translating data into a measurable instructional target can be inconsistent. The learning solution therefore emphasizes decision-making and practice rather than a compliance-only information dump.

Learner persona

“The Busy Case Manager”

Experienced enough to know the process, but balancing multiple students, meetings, data sources, and deadlines. Needs fast examples, clear decision rules, and reusable performance support.

Objectives

By the end, learners can…

  • Identify the instructional need supported by assessment evidence.
  • Distinguish measurable from vague goal statements.
  • Select an observable skill and criterion.
  • Draft a measurable annual goal.
Instructional strategy

Practice before perfection

Examples/non-examples → scenario → decision → feedback → guided rewrite → independent practice → downloadable job aid.

Storyboard sample

Screen 1 • Set the context
“Which statement tells us what the student needs to learn?”

Present a short student profile with assessment information. Ask the learner to identify the instructional need before showing any goal language.

Screen 2 • Choose the target
Decision point

“The student reads grade-level text with 60% accuracy. Which target is most appropriate?” Provide three choices and immediate feedback explaining why each option does or does not identify an observable skill.

Screen 3 • Build the goal
Guided practice

Drag/select the skill, condition, level of performance, and measurement method. The learner receives feedback before moving to the final practice item.

Knowledge check

Which is measurable?

A. Student will improve reading.
B. Student will increase oral-reading accuracy from baseline to the criterion established in the goal.

Feedback: B identifies an observable skill and a measurable criterion; A does not provide a clear performance target.

Job aid

Goal-writing quick check

  • Is the skill observable?
  • Is it connected to the documented instructional need?
  • Can performance be measured?
  • Is the criterion explicit?
  • Could another educator understand exactly what success looks like?
Evaluation

How success would be measured

Level 1: learner confidence/usefulness survey. Level 2: pre/post knowledge check. Level 3: review of sample goal-writing performance. Level 4: follow-up review of implementation quality after 30 days.

Privacy note

Portfolio-safe design

All examples are fictionalized. No student names, records, IEPs, or confidential school information are used.

02 / SUPPORTING STRUGGLING READERS

Intervention Basics for Paraeducators

Project type: Microlearning + facilitator-supported professional learning • Audience: Paraeducators supporting elementary learners

Performance goal: Paraeducators will consistently use a small set of evidence-informed support behaviors during reading intervention instead of relying on prompting, repetition, or task completion alone.
Need

Make intervention actionable

The training converts broad literacy guidance into observable actions paraeducators can use during a real intervention block.

Microlearning map

5-minute sequence

  1. Notice the learner behavior.
  2. Identify the skill being practiced.
  3. Select the least intrusive support.
  4. Check for understanding.
  5. Record the response and communicate it.
Scenario

What would you do?

A student repeatedly guesses at unfamiliar words. The learner chooses between giving the word, asking the student to try again, using a decoding prompt, or ending the task. Feedback explains the instructional purpose of each response.

Performance aid

Intervention pocket card

Prompt → Pause → Check → Record. A one-page reference for use during small-group reading support.

Facilitator guide excerpt

Opening: “Today we are not learning a list of reading strategies. We are practicing how to make the next instructional decision when a student gets stuck.”

Activity: In pairs, identify the skill, select the least intrusive support, and explain what evidence would tell you whether the support worked.

Debrief: Ask participants to name one support they can use tomorrow and one behavior they will stop doing because it prevents the learner from practicing independently.

Knowledge check

Best next step?

A student pauses on an unfamiliar word. Which response best preserves the learner's opportunity to practice the skill? Answer: Use a brief, targeted prompt and then allow the student to attempt the word.

Evaluation

Evidence of transfer

Observation checklist + self-reflection after two weeks + supervisor/teacher feedback on consistency of intervention behaviors.

03 / DESIGNING FOR LEARNERS WITH AUTISM

Predictable, Accessible, Actionable Learning

Project type: Inclusive-learning professional development demonstration • Audience: Educators and support staff

Performance goal: Staff will make practical environmental and instructional adjustments that increase predictability, communication access, engagement, and learner independence.
Learner-centered design

Design for access

The module uses short chunks, explicit navigation, visual organization, plain language, multiple ways to process information, and opportunities to practice decisions.

Decision framework

Observe → Hypothesize → Adjust → Measure

Staff begin with what they can observe, select a reasonable instructional/environmental adjustment, and monitor whether the learner's access or independence improves.

Scenario

“The transition isn't working.”

A learner becomes dysregulated during an unannounced transition. Participants choose between repeating the direction, adding a visual cue, reducing language, providing a predictable transition signal, or removing the expectation.

Accessibility

Built into the experience

  • Clear headings and predictable structure
  • Captions/transcripts for media
  • Text alternatives for visuals
  • Keyboard-friendly interaction concept
  • Minimal unnecessary animation

Sample learning screen

Pause & decide
“Before changing the expectation, ask: what barrier is preventing access?”

Choose one: unclear direction • unexpected transition • communication barrier • sensory/environmental demand • task mismatch.

Feedback: The goal is not to remove expectations automatically. The goal is to identify the barrier and select an adjustment that preserves meaningful participation whenever possible.

Job aid

Inclusive support checklist

  • Is the expectation clear?
  • Does the learner have a predictable way to know what happens next?
  • Is communication accessible?
  • Can the learner demonstrate understanding another way?
  • What data will show whether the adjustment helped?
Evaluation

From training to practice

Post-training confidence survey, scenario performance, implementation reflection, and a short follow-up observation focused on use of supports and learner independence.

04 / CORPORATE L&D DEMONSTRATION

First 30 Days: New Employee Onboarding

Project type: Corporate onboarding demonstration • Audience: New employees and their managers • Format: Blended onboarding journey

Business problem: New hires often receive large amounts of information at once but still do not know what “good performance” looks like, where to find help, or what they should accomplish first.
Learner persona

“The New Hire”

Motivated, information-heavy first week, uncertain about priorities, tools, stakeholders, and informal norms. Needs just-in-time guidance rather than a giant information dump.

30-day architecture

Four learning moments

  • Day 1: Orient and access.
  • Week 1: Perform core tasks.
  • Week 2: Apply and collaborate.
  • Day 30: Demonstrate independence and identify next development needs.
Manager support

Manager conversation guide

Managers receive three prompts: What should the employee be able to do independently? What does quality look like? Where should the employee go when blocked?

Performance support

“Where do I go?” job aid

A searchable-style one-page map connecting common new-hire questions to the correct system, person, process, or resource.

Sample onboarding module

Week 1 • Your first successful task
Scenario: You are ready to complete the task, but you are missing information.

Choose your next action: guess and submit • ask the manager immediately • check the documented process and then escalate the specific blocker • wait until the next team meeting.

Feedback: The preferred behavior is to use the documented process first and escalate a clearly defined blocker when needed. This reinforces independent problem-solving while protecting quality.

Assessment

30-day demonstration

The employee completes a realistic task, identifies the appropriate resources, explains the quality standard, and knows when/how to escalate a problem.

Evaluation

Business-facing measures

Time-to-productivity, completion of required onboarding tasks, manager confidence, new-hire confidence, common support requests, and 30/60/90-day retention or engagement indicators where available.
